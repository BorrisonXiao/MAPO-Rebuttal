Thank you for your thoughtful comments. We were glad to see that you share our concern about models losing track of the audio during long reasoning traces, the problem that motivated MAPO.

1. Given the small average accuracy gain, how significant are the method and results?

We agree that the significance of the method should be established through its effect on both task performance and audio use. In fact, our results do show consistent gains across benchmarks and seeds and, more importantly, a shift toward sustained use of acoustic evidence during reasoning, which is the behavioral change we aim to encourage with MAPO. The consistency across seeds and both fine-tuning settings supports a repeatable benefit over GRPO, averaging 2.30 percentage points with full fine-tuning and 1.91 with LoRA (Table 1). On MMSU, the 2.87-point gain over GRPO supports improved spoken-language understanding. The category breakdown further shows gains over Base Thinking in perception (+2.17 points) and reasoning (+4.01), suggesting better recognition and use of acoustic information (Appendix D).

Although absolute accuracy gains depend on the backbone's perceptual and reasoning capabilities, as discussed in Appendix J, our controlled analyses help identify the intended change in audio use. MAPO better preserves audio attention than Base Thinking at the transition into reasoning, after accounting for the growing text context (Appendix E.1). On the 200-example audio-essential MMAU subset, MAPO has higher clean accuracy than GRPO (67% vs. 60%) and a larger accuracy drop without audio (55 vs. 40 points), indicating greater audio dependence (Appendix E.2). Masking highly attended audio regions also causes a larger accuracy drop than masking an equal duration at random, supporting the relevance of the attended evidence (Appendix E.4). Together, these results support our claim that MAPO strengthens acoustic grounding during reasoning and improves the model's performance across audio benchmarks.

2. Are the gains mainly from instruction following? What are the MMAU-Pro results without that subset?

We agree that the instruction-following subset should be examined separately. Part of the gain on this subset can be attributed to the format reward that GRPO and MAPO share, since GRPO also improves on it, while MAPO improves further (95.40 vs. 90.80) and its advantage over GRPO does not depend on this subset. The subset contains 87 of the 5,304 scored MMAU-Pro questions (1.6%), and the table below gives the category scores together with the overall score recomputed without it.

3. Could KL divergence be used instead of the entropy-based signal?

We agree that KL divergence could also provide a useful reweighting signal. In fact, the two signals are strongly correlated in practice, supporting the entropy gap as a practical indicator of audio-conditioned distribution change. At a fixed checkpoint and with an identical text prefix, a nonzero entropy gap between audio-present and audio-removed predictions necessarily implies different token distributions and therefore positive KL divergence. Audio can make predictions either more concentrated or more uncertain. The absolute entropy gap captures both effects. KL also detects changes that preserve entropy, so their empirical agreement helps establish how well entropy tracks distribution changes during reasoning.

We compared the absolute entropy gap with exact KL(audio-present || audio-removed) using the base Qwen3-Omni-Thinking model on 100 MMAU questions, covering 45,702 token positions. Both conditions used identical saved response prefixes. The agreement holds across sound, music, and speech:

4. Can VPPO be applied to audio, and why is it not included as a baseline?

We agree that a comparison with VPPO is valuable. It was not included in the original manuscript because we recognized that transferring the algorithm from images to audio requires several critical design and implementation choices, particularly in how the audio should be corrupted. To address the reviewer's suggestion within the time and compute available for the rebuttal, we conducted a set of small-scale experiments on MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking, a smaller audio reasoning model from a different model family than the Qwen3-Omni model used in the paper. We trained for 128 steps with LoRA rank 16, alpha 32, keeping the original weights, audio encoder, and audio-to-language aligner frozen.

Transferring VPPO requires defining an audio counterpart to its image corruption. We occluded 50% of approximately 100 ms log-mel blocks before the audio encoder, replacing all frequency bins in each selected block with the clip's low-energy floor. This preserves audio length and prompt text. We retained VPPO's KL-based selection of the top 40% of response tokens and its trajectory-advantage shaping. All benchmark evaluations used clean audio.

MMSU 70.80 71.60 71.20 71.30

MAPO has the highest score on MMAU, MMAR, and MMAU-Pro, and it is the only one of the three methods that improves on the model without fine-tuning on all four benchmarks. Relative to audio-adapted VPPO, MAPO is ahead by 1.00 point on MMAU and 0.33 on MMAU-Pro, and the two are on par on MMSU and MMAR. Audio-adapted VPPO also improves over GRPO on MMAU, MMAR, and MMAU-Pro, which is consistent with the reviewer's expectation that token-level modality-aware optimization transfers to audio.

We agree that VPPO's optimization rule is modality agnostic, yet we also found in practice that the corruption mechanism it depends on is not. VPPO measures each token's dependence on the input by comparing predictions on the original input with predictions on a corrupted copy, and its published corruption masks patches on a fixed two-dimensional image grid. Audio does not offer a direct counterpart, because clips vary in length, information is carried over time, and the relevant evidence is often concentrated in a brief sound or a speaker transition. An audio version therefore requires choices about the masking axis (time, frequency, or both), the block duration, and the corruption strength, and under any fixed choice the same masking ratio can remove very different amounts of evidence from different clips. Since these choices determine which tokens VPPO selects, they also shape its updates. MAPO avoids this dependence by using a reference with the audio removed entirely, so the same definition applies unchanged to clips of any length and content, while its attention loss adds a second grounding signal computed directly from the model.

In the revision, we will expand the discussion of VPPO in Section 2 to distinguish its modality-agnostic optimization rule from the corruption mechanism it depends on, informed by the audio adaptation above. We will also include the comparison between the entropy gap and KL divergence and the MMAU-Pro category results in the appendix, and clarify the source of the instruction-following gain.

2. MMAR comparison with Audio-DeepThinker

What could be the reasons for the MMAR gap in results between MAPO and Audio-Deep Thinker?

Differences in the starting policy and reasoning supervision offer two plausible explanations for the MMAR gap. Audio-DeepThinker starts from Qwen3-Omni-Instruct, while MAPO starts from Qwen3-Omni-Thinking. In their own two-stage ablation, Audio-DeepThinker reaches 74.0% on MMAR from Instruct and 70.0% from Thinking, showing that the starting policy substantially affects performance under their recipe. MAPO starts from the Thinking model because it is designed to operate on an existing reasoning trace (Appendix I), and from this starting point it reaches 70.83%, compared with their 70.0%. Importantly, Audio-DeepThinker also rewards similarity to reference CoTs, providing guidance on the reasoning path and key steps. This additional supervision may benefit MMAR's challenging audio reasoning tasks. MAPO uses correctness, format, and reasoning-consistency rewards without reference-CoT supervision. These differences are documented in Audio-DeepThinker, Table 4 and Section 3.3.

Within our setup, MAPO improves MMAR accuracy from 66.90% for the base Thinking model and 68.43% for GRPO to 70.83%, supporting the benefit of modality-aware optimization for an existing reasoning policy. MAPO also achieves 79.35% on MMSU compared with 77.26% for Audio-DeepThinker, indicating complementary strengths across tasks. Together, these results support MAPO's contribution to improving audio reasoning through sustained use of acoustic evidence. In fact, as discussed in Section 2, MAPO "remains fully compatible with these sequence-level reward strategies." Audio-DeepThinker's reference-based rewards can guide the reasoning content alongside MAPO's token-level reweighting and attention objective.

For MAPO and GRPO, we report the mean and sample standard deviation across three seeds (17, 42, and 1234). 4. Mask-only ablation under full fine-tuning

In Table 1, it would be interesting to see the MAPO mask-only ablation results for full fine-tuning as well, is it possible?

The LoRA ablation in Table 1 gives the held-out effect of each branch. The modality relevance mask alone raises the four-benchmark average from 69.48% for GRPO to 70.46%, and adding the attention loss raises it further to 71.39%. The training trajectories in Appendices C.2–C.3 provide further evidence for the different roles of the two branches. Setting the attention-loss weight to \(\eta=0\) gives mask-only training, which reaches a training accuracy of 0.790, compared with 0.757 for GRPO, but produces shorter responses. At \(\eta=0.05\), adding the attention loss brings response length back near the GRPO level and increases mean audio attention from approximately 0.025 to 0.132. These settings show how the attention branch changes the balance between task-reward optimization and attention to the source audio.

Under full fine-tuning, MAPO improves the average from 70.95% for GRPO to 73.25%, showing that the complete method's benefit persists when all parameters are updated. We are unable to add a full fine-tuning mask-only run within the available rebuttal time and compute budget. The appendix trajectories nevertheless offer a useful view of the components' behavior: the mask improves task-reward optimization, while the attention branch counteracts the tendency toward shorter responses and encourages sustained audio attention.

5. Choice of reward weights

Are the default weights of the reward formulation ablated/explained? Defending or citing the choice of default values λ_acc=2, λ_format=1, and λ_cons=1 would strengthen the method design choices.

We selected the 2:1:1 weights through preliminary hyperparameter tuning rather than a full ablation, guided by the goal of prioritizing answer correctness while retaining incentives for structured and consistent reasoning. A well-formatted response or an internally consistent explanation can still give an incorrect answer, so these auxiliary criteria receive lower weights than accuracy, and the consistency term follows Audio-Thinker. Their role is to encourage the expected response structure and discourage contradictions or prematurely truncated reasoning. GRPO and MAPO are trained with the same reward and the same weights, so the comparison between the two methods is made under an identical reward. We will add this rationale to the experimental setup.

1. Effect of the frozen reference on the entropy signal and token weights

How do the masks and held-out results change when both entropy terms are evaluated with the same evolving checkpoint? Can you quantify how much of the fixed-reference signal is associated with policy drift rather than audio conditioning?

To separate the effect of audio from changes introduced by training, we measured next-token predictive entropy in three conditions: trained MAPO with audio, the same checkpoint without acoustic features, and the base model without acoustic features. The question, saved response prefix, and token positions were held fixed. Comparing MAPO with and without audio measures how audio changes the trained model's uncertainty. Comparing the two text-only predictions measures the entropy shift associated with training. An additional experiment gave the following mean absolute entropy differences:

Table 4. kaLY, 1. Effect of the frozen reference on the entropy signal and token weights
Comparison Mean absolute entropy difference
MAPO w/ versus w/o audio 0.445 nats
MAPO w/o audio versus base w/o audio 0.244 nats
MAPO w/ audio versus base w/o audio 0.622 nats

The text-only shift is therefore smaller than the within-MAPO audio/text effect, at about 55% of its magnitude. Because the reference entropy enters training only through the token weights of the two branches, we also examined how reference choice affects the resulting token weights in a separate replay of 100 MMAU questions and 44,269 saved response tokens, using the released MAPO weighting rule. At the final checkpoint, which is the furthest in training from the frozen reference, frozen-reference and same-checkpoint weights have Spearman rank correlations of 0.860 for the policy branch and 0.866 for the inverse-attention branch. Changing the reference redistributes 2.8% and 2.5% of normalized weight mass, respectively. This close agreement at the end of training indicates that the two choices of reference give similar training signals.

We use the frozen reference to reduce GPU memory overhead by reusing the model already maintained for GRPO's KL penalty, avoiding a separate entropy-reference model. A fixed reference also allows the text-only pass to be computed once per rollout batch and reused across the four optimizer updates (Appendix H.2), whereas a reference that follows the policy would need to be recomputed after every update. Given the smaller text-only shift and the strong agreement in normalized token weights, we regard this as a reasonable balance between preserving the weighting signal and limiting memory use and training time in our training setup.

2. Validating the entropy gap as an audio-dependence signal

Predictive entropy alone is too coarse to identify audio-dependent tokens. Because entropy is a single scalar summary, two completely different token distributions can have the exact same entropy value. A large gap in entropy does not necessarily mean a token truly depends on acoustic evidence. The metric works fine as a practical heuristic, but treating it as proof of true modality dependence requires stronger validation, such as a same-checkpoint audio ablation during training or a distribution divergence check.

Our same-checkpoint comparison with exact KL shows that the absolute entropy gap provides a useful signal for prioritizing tokens whose predictions respond to audio. At a fixed checkpoint and with an identical text prefix, a nonzero entropy gap between audio-present and audio-removed predictions necessarily implies different token distributions and therefore positive KL divergence. Audio can make predictions either more concentrated or more uncertain. The absolute entropy gap captures both effects. KL also detects changes that preserve entropy, so their empirical agreement helps establish how well entropy tracks distribution changes during reasoning.

We compared the absolute entropy gap with exact KL(audio-present || audio-removed) using the base Qwen3-Omni-Thinking model on 100 MMAU questions, covering 45,702 token positions. Both conditions used identical saved response prefixes. The agreement holds across sound, music, and speech:

All 0.865 [0.857, 0.873] 63.5%

3. Contributions of entropy reweighting and the attention branch

What are the results of attention-only training and of a simple uniform/POS-gated attention penalty under the same update budget? Does differential-entropy reweighting still add value under full fine-tuning?

The attention branch was designed on top of the mask-reweighting branch, motivated by our observation that the entropy gap correlates with the trend of audio attention decay during late-stage reasoning (Section 3.3). When the model stops using audio, the gap can become small and leave the mask with little signal, so the attention loss targets substantive tokens with small absolute entropy gaps and places greater weight on later CoT positions. Its intended effect is to restore audio use at these positions and increase their entropy gaps, allowing the mask to give them greater relative weight in subsequent policy updates. Thus, at a conceptual level, attention-only training without the mask is not a configuration the method is designed for, since the policy update would then weight all tokens uniformly and could not give greater weight to the positions where audio use is restored.

Similarly, a uniform attention penalty would spread optimization weight across all response tokens, including function words. The POS-gate ablation in Appendix G tests this directly under full fine-tuning: removing the gate lowers the four-benchmark average from 73.25% to 72.39%, with a lower mean on every benchmark. To test the attention-only case empirically, we ran a small-scale experiment on MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking, a smaller audio reasoning model from a different model family, training each variant for 128 steps with LoRA rank 16, alpha 32 under the same rollout and update budget. The attention-only variant adds the POS-gated attention penalty from the reviewer's question to GRPO, using uniform token weights in both the policy and attention losses so that entropy-based weighting is removed from both branches. The benchmark accuracies are as follows:

Benchmark GRPO Attention-only Full MAPO
MMAU 74.70% 75.20% 76.90%
MMSU 71.60% 70.30% 71.30%

Attention-only training reaches a four-benchmark average of 66.5%, on par with GRPO at 66.6%, while full MAPO reaches 67.8% and is above attention-only on all four benchmarks, by 1.0 to 1.7 points.

Together with the LoRA ablation in Table 1, where the modality relevance mask alone raises the four-benchmark average from 69.48% for GRPO to 70.46% and the full method reaches 71.39%, the MOSS study indicates that the gains do not come from attention regularization alone. Entropy-based weighting improves on GRPO without the attention loss, attention regularization without entropy weighting stays at the GRPO level, and the combination gives the best four-benchmark average on both model families. Under full fine-tuning, the available comparison is for the complete method, whose advantage over GRPO is maintained when all parameters are updated, with the four-benchmark average improving from 70.95% to 73.25% (Table 1).

4. Training initialization and response length

If MAPO and GRPO start the 29k-example stage from the identical AVQA checkpoint and use matched generation budgets, do the gains remain? Please report response lengths alongside evaluation accuracy to clarify the accuracy/computation trade-off.

MAPO's gain over GRPO is already present when the two methods start from the same initialization, as both of our preliminary comparisons do before the 29k-example continuation. Under LoRA, MAPO improves the four-benchmark average from 69.48% for GRPO to 71.39% (Table 1), and with full fine-tuning on AVQA it improves the average from 70.94% to 71.88% (Appendix C.1), with gains on all four benchmarks in both settings. The 29k-example continuation then examines how further training on a broader audio QA mixture builds on this gain, so we continue each method from its own AVQA-trained checkpoint to keep its optimization method consistent as the training data expands. The two same-initialization comparisons above therefore compare the methods without multi-stage effects.

The generation budgets are also matched, since MAPO and GRPO share the same rollout sampling setup, with eight completions per prompt, a generation batch of 768, and four updates per rollout batch. Both use generous completion-length limits in training, with the reported mean lengths well below those limits, and both are decoded with the same 2,048-token limit at evaluation, so response length is determined by each learned policy.

On response length, the 800-step training comparison in Appendices C.2–C.3 gives a mean completion length of 477.0 tokens for GRPO, and the MAPO trajectories show the following pattern:

Entropy reweighting alone produces a more compact response policy in this comparison, and a moderate attention weight brings completion length close to GRPO's while substantially increasing audio attention. The design of the attention branch helps explain this pattern. POS gating selects substantive tokens, including nouns, verbs, and modifiers that carry descriptions of sound sources, acoustic attributes, and events. The inverse entropy weights emphasize selected positions with small entropy gaps, while the temporal weight concentrates pressure later in the response. The attention penalty is averaged over POS-selected tokens, so η controls the strength of the source-attention signal at these positions. Our interpretation is that mask-only optimization favors a compact route to the answer, while stronger attention steering encourages continued description and comparison of acoustic evidence later in reasoning. This provides a linguistic account of the observed length changes in terms of the content the policy learns to sustain.

At η = 0.1, the value used in our main experiments, audio attention is highest and completions are about 10% longer than GRPO's (525.0 vs. 477.0 tokens). Because all auxiliary computations are training-only (Appendix H.2), response length is the only source of additional inference cost.

5. Scope of applicability to models with native reasoning

Improvements do not generalize beyond reasoning models. The reported performance gains are limited strictly to the CoT policy. Appendix I shows that applying the method to the Instruct policy actually leads to a slight performance drop on MMAU. While this negative result is useful to see, it contradicts the claim that the framework serves as a general-purpose optimization recipe across different model types, and I would suggest to tone down the claim.

We agree that the scope should be stated more clearly. MAPO is designed to improve audio grounding in models that already support native chain-of-thought reasoning, because its reweighting and attention objectives build on the reasoning trajectories provided by the starting policy, as discussed in the limitations (Appendix J) and shown by the Instruct-model analysis in Appendix I. Our additional experiments on MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking support this applicability across a second model family with native reasoning, complementing the Qwen3-Omni-Thinking results. In the revision, we will state the native-reasoning assumption alongside the applicability claim in the abstract, and describe the Instruct result in Appendix I as a slight drop.

Because the reference entropy enters training only through the token weights of the two branches, we also examined how reference choice affects the resulting token weights in a separate replay of 100 MMAU questions and 44,269 saved response tokens, using the released MAPO weighting rule. At the final checkpoint, which is the furthest in training from the frozen reference, frozen-reference and same-checkpoint weights have Spearman rank correlations of 0.860 for the policy branch and 0.866 for the inverse-attention branch. Changing the reference redistributes 2.8% and 2.5% of normalized weight mass, respectively. This close agreement at the end of training indicates that the two choices of reference give similar training signals.

We use the frozen reference to reduce GPU memory overhead by reusing the model already maintained for GRPO's KL penalty, avoiding a separate entropy-reference model. A fixed reference also allows the text-only pass to be computed once per rollout batch and reused across the four optimizer updates (Appendix H.2), whereas a reference that follows the policy would need to be recomputed after every update. Given the smaller text-only shift and the strong agreement in normalized token weights, we regard this as a reasonable balance between preserving the weighting signal and limiting memory use and training time in our training setup.

2. Audio-critical tokens and correctness of reasoning steps

Tokens in the same response still share the same sequence-level advantage. For example, an audio-related token could be correct, but receive a negative update because the final answer is wrong.

The example is helpful because it distinguishes audio relevance from semantic correctness. MAPO uses the audio/text entropy gap to identify critical positions and reweight their policy-gradient terms. The shared sequence-level advantage still determines the direction of those terms, so a negative advantage would also apply to the locally correct token in the reviewer's example. This advantage is, however, computed relative to the eight completions sampled for the same question (Section 2), so when the same token also appears in completions that score above the group average it is reinforced there, and across the group the update tends to concentrate on what separates the better responses from the worse ones. Providing separate feedback for that token would require a finer-grained semantic assessment of the generated CoT, such as step-wise process rewards that evaluate individual reasoning steps.

This form of supervision is complementary to MAPO's focus on acoustic grounding. Section 2 discusses MAPO's compatibility with sequence-level reward strategies. Step-wise CoT rewards could further refine which intermediate steps should be reinforced, while MAPO's weights determine their relative emphasis according to audio relevance. The two approaches can therefore guide the same policy optimization through complementary roles: reasoning supervision supplies finer-grained feedback on correctness, and MAPO directs learning toward audio-critical positions. We will add this discussion to the limitations in Appendix J.
